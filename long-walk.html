Track History — Worthington High, 1952

Here Ends His Long Walk

Bill Darling
Author
The 1952 Cardinals · Worthington, Ohio

The 1952 track team caught a break. Though they consisted of only 25 members — the smallest team Coach Eisenhart had ever fielded — he felt his team had the chance to be one of the best in school history. Due to the increasing population of the town and growing enrollment in the school system, Worthington had been moved up to Class A in 1951, making them one of the smallest “big” schools in the state, and they subsequently failed to score at the state meet for the first time in nine years. The number of boys in the school dipped just slightly in 1952, but enough to move the Cardinals temporarily back in with the small schools. Eisenhart had to know their long-term future was in Class A, but 1952 would be one of their last chances to be a big fish in a small pond.

The 1952 season began unofficially, and somewhat amazingly, on April 3, at Ohio Stadium. Coach Eisenhart, ever the salesman, somehow convinced Ohio State track coach Larry Snyder to host an exhibition tri-meet at The Horseshoe between Worthington High School, Columbus East High School, and the Ohio State University freshman track team. Given that college athletes (a) compete in different events, (b) throw heavier implements, and (c) go over higher hurdles, the idea of such a meet even being proposed is a bit absurd and today would likely be not only frowned upon by the OHSAA, but possibly banned. Thankfully, no team scores were kept as Worthington, predictably, did not win a single event. But the 1952 season was under way.

The first official, full-team meet of the 1952 campaign was on April 16 in Delaware, with host Willis High School (the predecessor to Delaware Hayes High) taking on Worthington and Arlington. The Cardinals came out on top, with senior Paul Mally winning both hurdle races, sophomore Jack Blackburn winning the mile, and Jack Bartley winning the pole vault in 9′ 6″. Two days later, Coach Eisenhart took a small contingent to the Mansfield Relays, where the boys placed third in the 4-mile relay and fifth in the 480y shuttle hurdles.

In the first two weeks of May, the Cardinals caught a wave and rode it to three straight invitational victories. On May 6, at the conference championships, Jack Blackburn and Jerry Williams took the first two spots in the mile, Jim Bivins and Cox matched that effort in the 880y run, and Paul Mally and Dick Rieke did the same in the long jump: three 1-2 finishes leading Worthington to its second straight Mid-Six conference title in front of the home fans. On May 11, the Cardinals then headed to Oxford and won the Miami Relays, buoyed by Jerry Cox taking the mile and Bob Stevens’ first place effort in the shot put with a throw of 43′ 3.5″. Then three days later, as described in the Dispatch, “Les Eisenhart’s classy Worthington High track team” won the seventh annual Upper Arlington relays by 9 points over Bellefontaine.

Worthington then returned to Delaware — this time to Ohio Wesleyan — for the district track championships on May 17. The Cardinals came home with six gold medals: Stevens in the shot put, Cox in the mile, Adams in the 440, Bivens in the 880, Jack Bartley in the pole vault, and the mile relay. Back home in Class B, Worthington made it to state in eight events, with two entries each in the mile, the 120y hurdles and the 880y run. As a team, they outclassed the field, winning the district championship by 15 points over second-place Richmond.

At the state meet at Ohio Stadium the following week, the Cardinals finished in eighth place, tied for the second-best team finish in history up to that point. Their state meet was highlighted by a fourth-place effort by Paul Mally in the 120y hurdles, a bronze medal for Bob Stevens in the shot put, and silver for the mile relay of Mally, Jim Bivens, Dick Rieke and Ken Adams. The Worthington News noted:

“This also marks the probable end of class B competition for the Cards, so next year will find them competing with the class A schools and thus the struggle will become tougher.”
— The Worthington News, 1952

Bob Stevens’ third place finish that day made history as it was the first time, and remains the only time, a male Worthington track and field athlete has scored at the state championship in the shot put. Mr. Stevens passed away on April 17, 2025, at the age of 91. His son, Dan — a former Worthington High track athlete and coach — is the strength and conditioning coach for both high schools in Worthington.

The Pole Vaulter

Jack Bartley being the 1952 district champion in the pole vault was a bit of a surprise, mostly because he had spent the autumn months of his senior year recovering from being shot.

On September 16, 1951, Bartley and his friends Jerry Cunningham, Arthur Loos and track teammate Paul Mally were all target shooting on a farm where Henderson Road meets the Olentangy River. In an unfortunate turn of events, Cunningham accidentally shot his best friend in the leg. The boys piled Bartley into an automobile and took him — not south to the nearby White Cross Hospital — but north back to Worthington. They took him to the McConagha Funeral Home, which doubled as the village’s ambulance service, perhaps in the hope that no hospital trip would be needed. The funeral home workers, understandably, took him directly to a hospital. Bartley’s profile in the 1952 yearbook indicates he did not run cross country for Coach Eisenhart in the fall of his senior year, for obvious reasons.

After graduation, Jack Bartley attended Ohio State University and began later working for the Columbus Transit Company, which operated the city’s public bus system. On April 30, 1955, he married the lovely Carole Lynn Kohler in a ceremony at St. Luke’s Lutheran Church. In July of 1962, the lovely Carole Lynn Kohler Bartley filed for divorce, seeking custody of the children. Divorced before he was 30, and having already survived being shot, Bartley gave up the life behind the wheel and turned to a career path familiar for someone in this situation: he became a country music singer.

Performing as the lead singer for bands such as The Workingmen, The Road Rangers, and the Shawnee Valley Boys, Jack Bartley performed in concert halls, county fairs, and clubs in and around central and southwest Ohio in the 1960s and 1970s. He was a regular at “Jessie’s Country Jamboree” in Waldo, the Circleville Pumpkin Show, and the “Paint Valley Jamboree” in Bainbridge, which was advertised as “The Biggest Show This Side of Nashville.” He released singles such as “Hot Pants” and “Cleaning Things Up” on the Country Giant music label and was the opening act for such Grand Ole Opry stars as Carl Butler and Pearl, David Huston and, most impressively, country music hall of famer Mel Tillis. Jack Bartley, who also had his own record store, was well known as a disc jockey in the Dayton area and, in 1971, was named the Ohio Country Music DJ of the Year by the Country Music Association in a ceremony held in Nashville, Tennessee.

The Distance Runner Who Changed His Name

The 1952 track team also had a junior distance runner by the name of David Bobrovnikoff. A cross country runner in the fall, he scored enough points in track meets in the spring of 1952 to earn a varsity letter. Incredibly bright, Bobrovnikoff scored 14th in Ohio in the chemistry portion of the state scholarship exam and was also a founding member of Worthington High’s first debating society. Accepted into Carnegie Mellon University out of high school, he attended medical school at the University of Pittsburgh. He also received advanced training in anatomic pathology at Duke Medical Center and the Scripps Research Institute in La Jolla, California, before joining the faculty at the UCLA College of Medicine. But when David Bobrovnikoff returned to Worthington in 1973 for his 20-year class reunion, in addition to all of his new titles, he also had a wife, a 1-year old son, and a brand new name: David Porter.

David Bobrovnikoff was the son of Nicholas Bobrovnikoff, who was simultaneously on the faculties of both Ohio Wesleyan and Ohio State, while also serving as the director of the Perkins Observatory in Delaware. A renowned astronomer, Bobrovnikoff was one of the world’s foremost experts on comets, especially Halley’s Comet. In 1919, German astronomer Karl Reinmuth discovered a new asteroid, four miles in length, and named it 2637 Bobrovnikoff in honor of his contributions to the field. But during the 1950s and 1960s, Dr. Nicholas Bobrovnikoff was part of research projects on the west campus of Ohio State University which were cloaked in secrecy. Funded by the U.S. military and the intelligence communities, the research focused on an issue that, until the late 1940s, wasn’t at all controversial: the shape of the earth.

Until the end of World War II, geodesy — the study of the earth’s shape — was one of the areas of science with the highest degree of cooperation between countries. In the words of University of California researcher John Cloud, “Historically, geodesy advanced cooperatively or not at all.” The Cold War changed all of that. With the drawing of the Iron Curtain and with America and Russia engaging in an arms race, accurately knowing every inch of the earth’s topography and curvature suddenly became a matter of national military security. From the late 1940s through the late 1960s, there were numerous top-secret research grants at Ohio State — hidden beneath innocuous-sounding and fictional topics such as soybean yields — that were actually geodesic grants funded by the Department of Defense. Professor Bobrovnikoff — an émigré from Russia and once an officer in the Russian imperial army — was well-versed in Russian geodesic research and was now applying that knowledge for the Americans on the west campus of Ohio State University, much to the dismay of the KGB.

When direct entreaties to Nicholas Bobrovnikoff to get him to stop had no effect, the KGB turned their attention to his family and began exerting pressure and imposing threats on Bobrovnikoff’s oldest son, David. In an attempt to release the pressure valve and throw Russian intelligence off of his tail, sometime during his trainings and relocations, he changed his name. For the rest of his professional career and personal life, he was known as David Dixon Porter.

On Sunday, September 6, 1970, David Dixon Porter went to the airport in Zurich, Switzerland and boarded Swissair flight 100, bound for New York City. Not long after the plane reached cruising altitude, an unfamiliar voice came over the address system and said, hauntingly:

“Good evening. You now have a new captain.”

The DC8 jetliner had been hijacked by Arab terrorists and was ordered to be flown to a remote desert airstrip in Zarqa, Jordan. Not long after landing, they were joined in the desert by TWA flight 741, which had originated in Frankfurt, Germany. In total, five planes were hijacked in five different countries. Three of them were forced to land in Zarqa, one landed in Cairo, and the last — El Al flight 219 from Amsterdam — managed to subdue their hijackers and land safely. It started a four-day saga that captivated the world.

The hijackers were from a group known as the Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine, or PFLP, and they made demands that Palestinian prisoners in Israel, Great Britain, America and West Germany be released over the next 72 hours, or the planes would be blown up with the passengers on board. On the second day, after negotiations with the Jordanian army, the hijackers agreed to let the women and children go, provided they were not Jewish. As the crisis reached day three, President Nixon considered a military air strike against the PFLP, but Secretary of Defense Melvin Laird scuttled the mission due to the weather. Years later Laird admitted “the weather” had just been an excuse to stop Nixon from making what Laird believed to be a terrible mistake.

On day 3, the hijackers blinked, extending the deadline by one day. They agreed to release another portion of the remaining hostages — provided they were not Jewish — who were whisked away to safety. Eventually, the PFLP deplaned all the remaining passengers and, in a last attempt at a show of strength, blew up all of the empty airliners. The PFLP then released more passengers but kept 54 Jews in a hidden location as political prisoners. Two weeks later, under siege by soldiers who were looking for them, the hijackers finally agreed to release all passengers, in exchange for the freeing of a handful of Palestinian prisoners, thus ending what journalist Martin Kolb called, “America’s introduction to global terrorism.”

Not a single passenger had been killed. In the end, the terrorists had been unwilling to commit mass murder to achieve their political ends. It was a mistake they would learn from and not repeat.

David Dixon Porter, M.D., was a professor of pathology at the UCLA College of Medicine for nearly thirty years. One of his specialties was training students in the art of performing autopsies and he also served as the deputy coroner for Los Angeles County. In homicide cases, Dr. Porter’s autopsy reports would be used as evidence in criminal proceedings and stored at the Superior Court Building, under the protection of the Los Angeles County Clerk who, from 1973 to 1976, was a fellow former Worthington High School track standout, Clarence Emerson Cabell.

David Dixon Porter died on April 16, 2017, from complications of Parkinson’s disease, at the age of 81.

Like his father before him, the details of his burial were never made public.

The Long Walk

Despite being but a sophomore in the spring of 1952, Jack Blackburn was the Mid-Six conference champion in the mile and finished second at the District, qualifying for the state meet. He would go on to win the Mid-Six mile in both 1953 and 1954 and, upon graduation, held the conference record with a time of 4:38.1. At the 1954 state meet, against Class A competition, he ran 4:31.0 to finish third, which remains the second-best placing by a Worthington runner at the state meet in the mile. Earlier in the same school year, in late October of 1953, Jack Blackburn became the first Worthington runner to win an OHSAA state championship in cross country, taking the 2-mile race held at the Ohio State University Scarlet and Gray golf course. Blackburn was, without question, one of the all-time great distance runners in Worthington history.

Jack Blackburn moved to the Springfield area where he worked as an alcohol and drug counselor, a high school teacher and, later, a professor at Urbana University. He started a nonprofit organization called Corinn’s Way, set up to help women and young parents get started on the right path in their life journeys. In 1984, he decided to run for public office and was elected as a commissioner for the city of Springfield, a position he held for three years.

Jack Blackburn ran track for the Ohio State Buckeyes during his early college days. After graduating, he stayed active in running, often taking part in masters track meets at Worthington High that were put on by the organization he helped found, the Ohio Track Club. He also, along with his father, transitioned into a more niche area of the sport by becoming one of the nation’s top competitive racewalkers. On July 3, 1960, Blackburn competed in the 50 kilometer racewalk at the U.S. Olympic Trials held in Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania. He finished fourth, only 200 meters out of third in the 31-mile, 5-hour race, missing the Rome Olympic team by an agonizing one spot. He competed in many Olympic trials through the years in racewalking and, all told, he finished in the top 6 three times, meaning he was a 3-time United States Olympic Team alternate. His fourth place result in 1960 is the highest ever finish at a USATF National Championship by a male Worthington track athlete and the closest any of them has ever come to competing in the Olympic Games. He would remain a staunch supporter of racewalking for the rest of his life.

John Coleman “Jack” Blackburn died on December 23, 2021, at the age of 86. He rests in Ferncliffe Cemetery in Springfield with a tombstone that features an image of his competition days and the following epitaph:

“Still the same loving, caring man he always was. He never strayed and here ends his long walk.”
