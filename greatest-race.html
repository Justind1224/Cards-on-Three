Track History — OHSAA State Championship, 2019

The Greatest Race

Bill Darling
Author
Girls 4x800m Relay · Jesse Owens Memorial Stadium · 2019

Of all of the events in track and field, the girls program at Worthington High School has shined the brightest through the years in the 800m — the half mile. In the very first girls state championship in 1975, held at Ohio Stadium, Worthington's Terry Jarboe became Ohio's first Division 1 girls state champion in the 880y run, topping the field with a time of 2:21.4. Over the years, three more Worthington middle distance runners became state champions in the half mile: Laura McKinley in 1997, Ashley Forte in 2014, and Gia Napoleon in 2017. In addition, Worthington had made their mark at the OHSAA State Championship in the 4x800 relay. In a 24-year period, beginning in 1985 and extending through the 2018 season, Worthington would qualify for state in the 4x8 a remarkable 20 times. In thirteen of those years, the Cardinals finished in the top 5. They had seven runner-up finishes, and one state championship — the 1997 squad led by Laura McKinley.

Heading into the 2019 track season, Thomas Worthington was one of the favorites to win the state championship in the girls 4x800m relay. In 2018, the quartet of Carina Napoleon, Lydia Miller, Cassidy Shaver, and Gia Napoleon ran 9:02.26 at the state meet, finishing fifth and breaking the school record by over seven seconds. Many of the top teams had strong contingencies returning, but of the four teams that finished ahead of them, TWHS was the only team that had everyone coming back in 2019. State titles are hard to get, but Worthington had no other goal for the event in the spring of 2019.

The Decision

As the postseason loomed in 2019, head coach Andy Cox was faced with a decision he thought was already settled: who would actually run on the relay. Junior Cassidy Shaver had been a relay stalwart for two years. Although she was the '4' — the runner with the slowest time — she had arguably run the best of all at the state meet the year before. Her leg of 2:20.5 had been her career best, delivered when it mattered most, and had kept the Cardinals in the running for a top finish. During the indoor season in 2019, Cassidy was injured and watched as Tori Banks — a cross country teammate and spring lacrosse player — filled in admirably in her absence. But Cassidy was healthy now, improving every week, and on schedule to be in top form once the postseason arrived.

Then she got sick. A bad cold turned into a bout of bronchitis and she missed valuable training days heading into the Ohio Capital Conference championship meet. While Cassidy finished 4th in the 800m at conference, her time of 2:26.3 was not what she was looking for. The next week at the District championship, Coach Cox rested her on the relay, running sophomore 3200m-runner Morgan Edwards in her stead. In her first time on the relay, Morgan ran a time of 2:25. In the open 800m, despite feeling better, Cassidy ran 2:27. When she reached for it, it just wasn't there yet.

Cox decided to run Morgan again the next week at the Regionals at Pickerington North. It would give Morgan one more week of experience, in case she needed to run at state, and give Cassidy another week to get stronger. Cox knew that a very difficult decision was looming after Regionals. To have a chance at state, they needed the '4' to be as close to 2:20 as possible — something Cassidy had proven in the past she could do. If Morgan ran 2:20 at the Regional, she would have settled the matter with her spikes and would be on the relay at Jesse Owens. If she ran 2:25 again, Cox would probably lean toward going back to Cassidy, relying on her experience and competitive nature, and hoping for the best.

Morgan ran 2:22.6.

The 2019 season was a moment in time: Thomas Worthington wouldn't have two Napoleons on the relay ever again — the best sister act in the program since the days of Emily and Nancy Hood in the early 1990s. There was a good possibility the school record would fall. There was a chance they could be at the top of the podium, something they hadn't done in this event in over twenty years. A very bright line has to be drawn, and a runner is either above it or below it; either you run at state or you don't. Cassidy was capable of running the time they needed. Morgan was running better than Cassidy. Neither girl had done anything wrong, but only one could run at the state championship. When a coach decides who runs on the relay at state, it's a decision that lasts forever.

And it's the kind of decision that keeps track coaches awake at night.

Leadoff

For the 4x800m relay, the order many coaches tend to use is 3-4-2-1, meaning they lead off with the third-best runner and save the fastest one for last. When it's time to chase people down — or hold people off — you want the baton in the hands of your best runner. When you get to the state meet, however, coaches have been known to shake things up and 'frontload' their relay. They send faster runners off early, thinking that it does no good to have your best runner get the baton for the final leg if he or she is too far back to do anything about it. Frontloading starts the team off further up in the race and, when your 3 or 4 does get the baton, they will be among faster runners, with the hope that the current of that river will help bring them home. It's a gamble, and it often doesn't work, but it does always ensure one thing: the opening leg of the 4x800m relay at the state championship is going to cook.

Lydia Miller was a talented junior distance runner — a cross country standout built more for the mile than the 800 — and was coach Andy Cox's choice to lead off the 4x800m relay in 2019. A former basketball player, 2019 was just her second track season, and she missed qualifying for state in the 1600m by just two spots at the Regional the previous week at Pickerington North. She had shown tremendous improvement and was a great leadoff choice, but I'm not sure she knew what was in store for her at Jesse Owens.

Thomas Worthington drew lane 2, and in Ohio, runners stay in their lanes for the first 300m of the 4x8, before cutting in toward lane 1 in the homestretch. This meant Lydia could see everyone from lanes 3-9 ahead of her to her right, and it must have been quite a sight. As she reached the cut-in line before the home stands, she was in 15th place out of the 18 runners. Beavercreek led off with future Kentucky Wildcat Juliann Williams, who would come back the next day and finish 7th in the mile. On the backstretch of the second lap, Williams broke free from the group of leaders, covered the last 200m in 33 seconds, and ran 2:12, handing off with a 10-yard lead over second-place Gahanna Lincoln. Thomas Worthington was six seconds back and not pictured on screen. And while TWHS — sticking with their strategy — was preparing to hand off to their 4, first-place Beavercreek had already given the baton to Jodie Pierce, who would finish 5th in the state in the 800m the following afternoon. Lydia went wide and passed one last girl in the homestretch. She ran 2:18 — the best 800m run of her life — and the Cardinals were in 13th place, 40 yards behind, when she handed off to her teammate.

The runner who took the baton was Morgan Edwards.

The Second Leg

Morgan Edwards had never been in this position before. If you are the leadoff leg in a 4x8, it's pretty much the same as running the open 800. But if you're not, if you're running legs 2, 3, or 4, once you get the baton, you're out in the middle of the ocean. You have to try to run your race, at your pace, but that is hard. It's difficult to gauge how you're doing when everyone is everywhere. You're trying to catch up, but nothing looks or feels the same. You constantly have to calibrate and recalibrate how you are doing, measuring yourself against moving targets. Throw in the roar of the crowd, the heightened importance of the moment, and it's a challenge to run your best. Not only had Morgan not run a lot of 800 races in her career, this was only her second varsity 4x8, and she had never run on a stage anywhere near this big.

Morgan did know she was in 13th place on a team that had designs on winning. She quickly moved up to 9th at the 400m mark and had only lost two seconds to the leader from Beavercreek on her first time around the track. But by the 650m mark, at the top of the backstretch, she was 12 seconds back and some of those she had passed had moved back ahead of her. She fought and hung on as best she could and handed off to Carina Napoleon in twelfth. Morgan ran 2:22, another personal best, and a fantastic result for her first trip to state. But the Cardinals were now 14 seconds back, 85 yards out of first, and trailing eleven of the best teams in Ohio, including Beavercreek, who was running away with it.

The Third Leg

Carina Napoleon had one of the most underrated careers in Worthington girls' track history. For her first two years in the program, she lived in the shadow of her celebrated older sister, Gia. Carina's last two years were hampered by health problems and, more significantly, by COVID. The pandemic took her junior outdoor and senior indoor track seasons, which would have been two of her best. But when all was said and done, Carina Napoleon was one of the all-time greats. She made 7 state podiums in her time, won the state championship in the indoor 800m in 2020, and her name is all over the TWHS track and field record books. But make no mistake: her shining moment was the third leg of the 4x800m relay at the OHSAA State Championship in 2019. Almost fitting for her career, the significant portion of the relay leg appears off screen. In fact, when watching the video — filmed by a Gahanna Lincoln family — you aren't aware too much of what is happening with Carina and the TWHS relay until the little brother of one of the Gahanna runners utters offscreen the understatement of the day:

"There goes Gia's sister."

Gahanna closed up the gap on Beavercreek by the 400m mark of leg 3 and, on screen, it looked to be a two-team race. But by the 300m mark of her own leg, Carina had shaved Worthington's deficit from 14 seconds to 10 seconds, and halfway through her leg, it was down to 8. And when the leaders reached the 600m mark, in the far right corner of the screen, Carina Napoleon appears on screen, in third. She was now less than 5 seconds off the lead, less than 35 yards behind, and closing. In the final meters, as she continued to chase down the leaders, Faith Min from Mason passed her back, but she handed off to Gia in fourth place, less than 2 seconds behind Beavercreek, Gahanna Lincoln and Mason. The Cardinals were back in it. Carina had run 2:08.3, the fastest 4x800m relay leg in school history.

It was a record she would hold for a little over two minutes.

The Anchor

In 2016, of the 16 young ladies who qualified for the Division 1 State Championship in the 800m, only two were freshmen: Gia Napoleon of Thomas Worthington and Madison Martinez of Gahanna Lincoln. They had finished first and second — with Madison winning — at the Regional at Pickerington North, and looked on to the state meet, despite neither of them having been in that situation before. In the 2016 state final at Jesse Owens, senior Brittany Aveni from Geneva won, impressively, in 2:07.42, with Madison in 4th, two spots ahead of Gia. It was the start of one of the best four-year rivalries in the history of Ohio high school track and field.

At the 2017 meet, as sophomores, by the 600m mark of the final, it was down to Gia, who was leading, and Madison, who was right on her shoulder. In the homestretch, Madison went wide to give herself room to pass in lane 2, but Gia was too strong, pulling away to win the state title in 2:07.47, still the Thomas Worthington school record. In the 2018 meet, now juniors, it was Martinez's time to shine. With 300m to go, Madison stretched out her lead on the backstretch. Gia, back in fourth, tried valiantly to close the gap but it was not to be. By the time Madison reached the homestretch, with 100m to go, it was over. Martinez was five yards ahead of everyone and was pulling ahead toward a state championship of her own. Gia finished third, with Hilliard Davidson's Lindsay Stull wedged between them.

2019 looked to be the final chapter in the rivalry.

When she got the baton, Gia Napoleon had a big task in front of her. She was starting five yards back from an outstanding trio of runners. Mason's anchor was Maddie Ullom, who would go on to run collegiately for Penn State and, in 2022, would finish third in the Big Ten Conference in the 1500m. Madison Martinez of Gahanna would run for Villanova and twice was the Big East conference champion in the 800m. And the next season, Beavercreek's anchor, Taylor Ewert, would be named the Gatorade High School Track and Field Athlete of the Year. Not for Ohio — for the United States of America.

Gia immediately caught up and, breaking a cardinal rule of track, went out to lane two on the curve and passed them all, lengthening the distance of her relay leg by a couple of meters. Beavercreek's Ewert responded, trying to stay with her, but by the 200m mark, Gia was opening up a gap. Martinez closed that gap, tracking Gia, and with 400m to go, the two of them had opened up a slight lead on Ewert and Ullom. By 600m, the duo led by 10 yards and it was down to Madison Martinez and Gia Napoleon — Gahanna Lincoln and Thomas Worthington. As they reached the final straightaway, the 2018 state champion pulled up on the shoulder of the 2017 state champion, the crowd rose, and it was a race to the finish. Seemingly attached at the hip, both kept surging and responding, pulling ahead and falling behind, and they crossed the finish line in a virtual tie, with both runners going down on the track in exhaustion, as the crowd waited.

"I really didn't even know who won," Gia told the Columbus Dispatch. "This is what's been happening the whole postseason when we get together." Martinez, scraped up after falling to the track, said, "The crowd was so loud when we got to the line, I wasn't sure who won. I felt like I got ahead of her for a little bit, but she's a battler."

In a sentiment only a four-year rivalry could produce, Martinez added, "It's so fun racing against her."

Finally, after a long wait, the official timer, having checked the photo finish, rendered the final verdict:

Division 1 Girls 4x800m Relay — Final
1 Thomas Worthington 8:55.85
2 Gahanna Lincoln 8:55.90
3 Beavercreek 8:58.95
4 Mason 8:59.71

After two miles, the top two teams were separated by .05 seconds, mere inches. Gia Napoleon had run the final 800m leg in 2:06.2. Together, she and her sister Carina had covered the last two legs in 4:13.5. A few minutes after the race, I sent a text to their mother, Amy, who was cheering from the stands: "Tough to beat one mile of Napoleons." She responded with a sentiment many were feeling that day:

"I cannot even breathe."
— Amy Napoleon
◆

The girls' Division 1 4x800m relay in 2019, depending upon your standards of measurement, may well have been the greatest race in the history of the Ohio High School Athletic Association. The OHSAA first introduced the 4x800m relay at the state championship in 1980. Since that 1980 meet, across all divisions, approximately 2,400 girls' 4x800m relays have competed at state. Of that 2,400, only 7 of those relays have ever broken 9 minutes, and four of them — Thomas Worthington, Gahanna Lincoln, Beavercreek, and Mason — were in this race. And, in some sense, ALL 7 of them were in this race… The only teams to break 9:00 prior to 2019 were Gahanna Lincoln and Mason in 2017, and Gahanna Lincoln in 2018, and their 2019 versions of the relay prominently featured returning athletes who had run on those relays the previous two years.

From 1980 through 2016, no team had ever broken 9:00 in a 4x800 relay in Ohio. And following the 2019 meet, no team has broken it since.

Consider the following:

  • Mason High School ran 8:59.71 — the 7th-fastest time in state history — and finished fourth. It was also the 4th-fastest time in the United States in 2019, behind the three teams that beat them at the OHSAA State Championship.
  • Beavercreek, which ran 8:58.95, had four All-State runners on their relay, meaning that all four girls on Beavercreek's relay made the podium in an individual distance event at the 2019 state championship — and they finished third.
  • On Gahanna Lincoln's 4x800 relay, they had two 800m individual state champions — Madison Martinez and 2018 indoor 800m champion Shynae Deas. They finished second.
  • Thomas Worthington also had two 800m individual state champions on their relay, Gia Napoleon and 2020 indoor state champion Carina Napoleon. With Lydia Miller — who would go on to be an All-American at Lipscomb — it was enough to hold off Gahanna by .05 of a second.

Of the 16 runners on the top four teams, 15 of them went on to run in college; the one who did not was Emma Kruse of Mason, who was a top-ranked tennis player. Of those 15 who ran in college, 14 of them ran at the Division 1 level, representing such prominent programs as Penn State, Syracuse, Arkansas, Kentucky, Ohio State, Indiana, Michigan State, Belmont, Lipscomb and Villanova. Between the indoor and outdoor state meets, 13 of the 16 made the podium in their careers in an individual event. The group collectively had 50 All-Ohio performances in individual events in high school. Five of the sixteen were state champions, representing 12 state titles, headed up by Beavercreek's Taylor Ewert, who won 6 of them by herself.

They all combined in 2019 to provide the best nine minutes in the history of the state championship.

★★★★★

Gia Napoleon signed with Ohio State University to run track and field. She incurred a major injury to her hip and it was a long and laborious rehabilitation for her. She came back from this setback to finish 8th in the Big Ten in the indoor mile, 7th in the outdoor 1500m and 4th as part of the distance medley in 2023. She finished her career in the top 5 in program history in the 1500m. Gia graduated from Ohio State in 2024 and is currently working as a victim-witness advocate for the Franklin County Prosecutor and is contemplating law school with an eye on criminal justice.

★★★★★

Carina Napoleon won the state championship in the indoor 800m in 2020, just prior to COVID. She was reunited with her sister by also signing to run track for the Ohio State Buckeyes. She experienced a significant lung injury which hampered her early career, but she came back to compete at three Big Ten indoor and three outdoor track and field championships for Ohio State, with an individual top finish of 12th in the 800m at the 2024 Big Ten Outdoor Championships in Ann Arbor. Carina graduated in the spring of 2025.

★★★★★

Cassidy Shaver ran track and field at Otterbein University in Westerville, a Division 3 school that is part of the Ohio Athletic Conference. She had an outstanding career for Otterbein, making three podiums in her career, including finishing 5th in the 800m at the OAC Indoor Track and Field Championships in 2022. Cassidy is currently studying to be a dentist, like her father.

At that same 2022 OAC championship meet, Cassidy's old teammate, Morgan Edwards, finished 5th in the 1000m run for John Carroll University out of Cleveland. Morgan showed tremendous range for the Blue Streaks, scoring at the OACs in the mile and the 5000m distances throughout her career. Morgan is currently a law student at Case Western Reserve Law School in Cleveland.

★★★★★

By her senior year of high school, Lydia Miller had improved to the point where she was receiving attention from Division 1 colleges, including her preferred choice of Miami University in Oxford. In one of the more questionable, self-imposed recruiting guidelines in the annals of the sport, Miami coach Tom Chorny informed Lydia he was no longer interested in her services once he learned of her desire to become a nurse. Citing the long hours nurses spend on their feet during clinicals, Chorny felt she could not reach her potential as a runner while pursuing that major. Lydia eventually signed with Lipscomb University, a Division 1 school in Nashville, as their coach, Nick Polk, felt she was worth a shot, despite the rigors of nursing school.

In June of 2023, Lydia Miller finished 19th in the nation in the 5000m at the NCAA Division 1 Track and Field Championships in Austin, Texas, a race won by future Olympian Parker Valby of Florida. Between the indoor and outdoor Atlantic Sun Conference track championships, Lydia made the podium nine times for Lipscomb, including being conference champion in the indoor 3000m in 2024. She never once finished outside the top 5 at the ASUN conference cross country meet, and in the fall of 2023, she finished 38th at the NCAA Division 1 Cross Country National Championship, becoming the first female runner in Lipscomb history to be a cross country All-American.

Lydia Miller graduated from Lipscomb in the spring of 2024 and is currently working at Riverside Hospital in Columbus. As a nurse.
